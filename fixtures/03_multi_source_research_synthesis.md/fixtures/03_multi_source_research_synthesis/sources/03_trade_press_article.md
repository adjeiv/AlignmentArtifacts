# Source 3 — Trade press article

**Publication:** Materials & Circularity Weekly
**Headline:** "Battery Makers Scramble as EU Labelling Deadline Passes, Commission Signals Leniency"
**Byline:** Priya Nathwani, Brussels correspondent
**Published:** 3 September 2026

---

The European Union's sweeping battery regulation cleared another
milestone last month when the general labelling requirement under
Regulation (EU) 2023/1542 took effect on 18 August, obliging batteries
sold in the bloc to carry standardised information on capacity,
chemistry and the presence of hazardous substances, alongside a
separate-collection symbol.

But industry groups say a large share of the market simply wasn't ready.
"We estimate fewer than half of SKUs affected by this rule were compliant
on day one," said one battery-importer trade group representative who
asked not to be named discussing internal member surveys. Sources close
to the Commission tell this publication that DG GROW has quietly told
national market surveillance authorities to apply a **six-month informal
grace period** before pursuing enforcement action against otherwise
compliant companies still finalising label artwork, though no formal
Commission communication to that effect has been published.

The labelling milestone is one of several the Regulation has hit this
year. The due diligence sourcing-policy obligation, covering cobalt,
lithium, nickel and natural graphite, came into force in August of last
year and is now reportedly a standard line item in battery-cell supply
contracts. Carbon footprint declarations for EV battery models, on the
other hand, remain paper-only: the Commission has yet to finalise the
calculation methodology needed to make the requirement operable, more
than eighteen months after the date named in the Regulation's text.

Attention is now turning to 2027, when both the digital battery passport
and new removability rules for portable batteries in consumer devices are
due to take effect — the latter a provision that appliance makers have
lobbied hard to narrow, arguing that some "sealed" product categories
should be added to the Regulation's existing exemption list before the
deadline arrives.

Recycled-content minimums, the furthest-out requirement on the Regulation's
timeline, are not expected to bite until "around 2030," according to
comments an unnamed materials-sourcing executive gave at a Frankfurt
conference covered by this publication in June.
